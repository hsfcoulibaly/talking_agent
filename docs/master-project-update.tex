\begin{abstract}
Modern interactive media frequently struggles with ludonarrative dissonance~\cite{Hocking2007}, where dynamically driven player actions conflict with static, pre-scripted game narratives. While recent advancements in Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have enabled narrative extraction from single, static images, these approaches lack the temporal and sequential context required to interpret complex gameplay. This project introduces a novel, multi-modal machine learning pipeline designed to generate context-aware, dynamic dialogue for Non-Playable Characters (NPCs) based on sequential gameplay screenshots.

The proposed architecture separates visual perception from narrative generation to strictly ground the AI in gameplay reality. First, a VLM sequentially processes in-game images to extract key entities, actions, and environmental states, structuring this data into a chronological scene graph. Second, a Large Language Model (LLM) utilizes this structured data---constrained by established interactive storytelling principles derived from Adams~\cite{Adams2014}---to generate dramatically meaningful and credible NPC dialogue. By filtering raw visual data into a strict JSON framework before text generation, the pipeline reduces LLM hallucination and arbitrary content creation.

The pipeline was evaluated on six \emph{Minecraft} runs stratified by context length (5, 10, and 15 minutes of gameplay, two runs each). Stage~1 VLM extraction was assessed across 1{,}440 binary labels (360 frames $\times$ 4 conditions), yielding F1 scores of 1.00 for action description and player-status extraction, and 0.88 and 0.85 for location detection and entity identification respectively, with false positives concentrated in menu/inventory frames. Stage~3 dialogue quality was evaluated by two independent LLM judges from different model families---Gemini~2.5~Flash~\cite{GeminiTeam2024b} and Groq/Llama-3.3-70B~\cite{Dubey2024}---alongside a two-rater human study. Both judges independently identified Grounding as the weakest dimension (Gemini avg 4.67/5; Groq avg 4.17/5) while agreeing that Coherence and Arbitrary Content are perfect (5.00/5.00). Human raters largely concurred on Grounding (4.67) but rated Emotional Richness (3.33) substantially lower than either automated judge, suggesting LLM-based judges overestimate emotional quality relative to human perception. Results also indicate that longer context windows produce higher-quality narratives, with 15-minute runs receiving consistently higher scores than 5-minute runs from all three evaluators.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The landscape of interactive media and video games has evolved from simple, linear progression into expansive, open-world environments where player agency dictates the flow of events. As rendering technologies and physics engines have advanced to create highly realistic simulations, the systems governing in-game storytelling have often lagged behind. In most contemporary open-world games, Non-Playable Character (NPC) dialogue remains heavily pre-scripted. While branching dialogue trees offer the illusion of choice, they are ultimately finite and struggle to account for the emergent, unpredictable actions players take in a physics-driven world.

This master's project implements a prototype \emph{talking agent} pipeline: a batch of time-ordered gameplay screenshots is summarized per frame by a VLM, merged into a single structured \emph{scene graph}, and passed to an LLM that produces short NPC dialogue under explicit grounding constraints. The implementation uses a configurable Google Gemini model~\cite{GeminiTeam2024b} (default \texttt{gemini-2.5-flash}; overridable via \texttt{GEMINI\_MODEL} in \texttt{.env} or the \texttt{--model} flag) for both the vision and language calls, with extraction forced to \texttt{application/json} for machine-parseable state.

A key design goal is evaluation completeness. Prior work on VLM pipelines often evaluates only the perception stage. This project contributes a two-track evaluation: automated F1 for Stage~1, and a dual LLM-as-judge scoring method~\cite{Zheng2023} for Stage~3 whose dimensions are grounded in Adams's theory of game storytelling~\cite{Adams2014}. Adams defines a good game story as an account of dramatically meaningful events that is credible, coherent, without undue repetition, and without arbitrary content, and identifies length, characters, degree of realism, and emotional richness as key quality factors. These criteria directly motivate the seven scoring dimensions used in this project's dialogue evaluation. To study the effect of context length on narrative quality, the evaluation is stratified by gameplay duration: 5, 10, and 15 minutes of \emph{Minecraft} footage at a 5-second frame extraction interval, with two runs at each duration level.

\section{Background}

A Vision-Language Model (VLM) processes visual data, and allows text to be generated,
such as image captioning~\cite{bordes2024introductionvisionlanguagemodeling}.
One way to create a VLM is to pair an image encoder with an LLM.
``Grounding VLMs is another important step to ensure
that the VLMs correctly associate words with specific concepts''~\cite{bordes2024introductionvisionlanguagemodeling}.
A prototype system for AI \& Interactive Storytelling in Cultural Heritage was developed for the Dunhuang Murals (specifically the Jataka stories) to move beyond passive viewing~\cite{11227644}. The system includes mural segmentation to explain individual elements, interactive knowledge graphs that trigger cinematic visual effects when clicked, and a creative reconstruction tool where users build their own comic-style narratives. An AI agent (built on the COZE platform and GPT models) acts as an approachable guide, providing real-time explanations and creative suggestions while drawing from a curated knowledge base to prevent ``hallucinations''. User testing showed high scores in attractiveness, novelty, and stimulation, suggesting the system helps users form deeper emotional and intellectual connections with heritage.

Recent work has also applied large foundation models directly to video-game environments. Voyager~\cite{Wang2023Voyager} uses GPT-4 as a lifelong agent in \emph{Minecraft}, autonomously learning skills through code-based actions without access to visual screenshots. CRADLE~\cite{Tan2024CRADLE} takes a complementary approach: it operates from raw screen pixels using a VLM, enabling computer control without any game-specific API. Unlike these agent-oriented systems, the present project focuses on the \emph{narrative layer}---using sequential screenshots not to act in the game world but to understand it well enough to generate contextually grounded NPC dialogue.

\section{System Architecture and Implementation}

\subsection{Design goals}

The design follows three principles aligned with the research abstract: (1)~\textbf{temporal grounding}---images are processed in a user-defined order (e.g., lexicographic filename order as a proxy for capture order); (2)~\textbf{structured mediation}---raw pixels are never passed directly to the dialogue model; only JSON derived from vision passes through; (3)~\textbf{constrained narration}---the final prompt instructs the model to avoid inventing entities, locations, or plot beats absent from the scene graph.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  % \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{architecture_diagram.png}
  \caption{Overview of the proposed multi-image AI pipeline.}
  \label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

Visuals are critical to understanding the data flow through the system. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}, the system processes gameplay screenshots through distinct extraction, consolidation, and generation stages.

\subsection{Stage 1: Per-frame visual extraction}

For each frame index $i = 0,1,\ldots$, the pipeline sends the screenshot and a fixed instruction prompt to the VLM. The model must return a JSON object with fields including \texttt{location}, \texttt{time\_of\_day}, \texttt{player\_status}, \texttt{recent\_action}, \texttt{notable\_enemies\_or\_objects}, and \texttt{visible\_entities} (a list of short strings). Extraction uses a low temperature (0.2) to favour factual stability. Each result is wrapped with metadata: \texttt{frame\_index}, \texttt{source\_image}, and \texttt{observation}.

\subsection{Stage 2: Consolidation into a chronological scene graph}

Per-frame observations are merged into one JSON \emph{scene graph} suitable for dialogue generation. Two modes are implemented:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{LLM consolidation (default).} A second call to the same model merges the ordered frame list into a graph with \texttt{timeline\_summary}, \texttt{environment} (\texttt{location}, \texttt{time\_of\_day}, \texttt{conditions}), \texttt{entities} (with \texttt{id}, \texttt{name\_or\_description}, \texttt{type}, \texttt{first\_frame}, \texttt{last\_frame}), \texttt{events} (\texttt{frame\_index}, \texttt{description}, \texttt{involved\_entity\_ids}), and \texttt{player\_arc} (\texttt{status\_notes}, \texttt{notable\_actions}). The consolidator is instructed not to invent content absent from the observations.
  \item \textbf{Simple stitch (\texttt{--simple-consolidate}).} A deterministic merge (no extra API call) concatenates actions, collects entity strings, and builds a minimal graph; useful for cost-sensitive runs or ablations.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Stage 3: NPC dialogue generation}

The LLM receives only the consolidated scene graph and a block of \emph{storytelling constraints} aligned with the interactive storytelling principles derived by Adams~\cite{Adams2014}: ground all claims in the JSON; target 80--120 words; avoid game-UI elements (health bars, menus, HUD text); express genuine emotion; and avoid repeating the same idea. Dialogue generation uses a higher temperature (0.7) for phrasing variety while the input remains fixed structured state.

Game UI elements are intentionally excluded from the prompt constraints to prevent the narrative from breaking the fourth wall. Figure~\ref{fig:ui_comparison} shows a side-by-side view of a frame with and without UI to make this distinction clear. By ignoring health bars, quick-bars, and crosshairs, the AI is forced to contextualize only the immersive elements of the environment.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Dataset}

A 24-minute 21-second \emph{Minecraft}~\cite{Johnson2016} gameplay session was recorded and frames were extracted at a 5-second interval, yielding 292 total frames. Six structured runs were produced by varying context length:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{5-minute runs} (60 frames, 2 runs)
  \item \textbf{10-minute runs} (120 frames, 2 runs)
  \item \textbf{15-minute runs} (180 frames, 2 runs)
\end{itemize}

For each duration group, the first run executed the full three-stage pipeline (VLM extraction + LLM consolidation + dialogue generation). The second run reused the VLM-extracted frames from the first and ran only the LLM stages, testing output consistency given identical visual input.

\section{Evaluation}

Evaluation spans all three pipeline stages. Stage~1 is evaluated with automated binary classification metrics across all extracted frames. Stage~3 is evaluated with a dual LLM-as-judge method~\cite{Zheng2023} using two independent model families and a human rating study. Stage~2 (scene graph consolidation) is assessed indirectly through the Grounding dimension of the Stage~3 judge: if consolidation omits or distorts key entities, the dialogue scores will reflect it.

\subsection{Evaluation framework: Adams's storytelling criteria}

The dialogue evaluation framework is derived from Adams~\cite{Adams2014}, who defines a good game story as an account of dramatically meaningful events satisfying five qualities: \textbf{credibility}, \textbf{coherence}, absence of \textbf{undue repetition}, absence of \textbf{arbitrary content}, and \textbf{dramatic meaning} (engagement). Adams also identifies \textbf{length}, character presence, \textbf{degree of realism}, and \textbf{emotional richness} as key story factors. These criteria map directly onto the seven scoring dimensions shown in Table~\ref{tab:dimensions}.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Dialogue scoring dimensions and their source in Adams~\cite{Adams2014}.}
\label{tab:dimensions}
\begin{tabular}{>{\bfseries}lp{7.5cm}}
\toprule
Dimension & Definition (1--5 scale) \\
\midrule
Grounding         & All concrete claims traceable to the scene graph; no invented entities or events. \\
Coherence         & Internally consistent, logically ordered, single narrative voice. \\
Credibility       & Believable within the game universe; tone and vocabulary fit an NPC companion. \\
Repetition        & Free from undue repetition of ideas or phrases (5 = no repetition). \\
Arbitrary Content & No irrelevant game-UI elements such as health bars, menus, or HUD text (5 = none). \\
Emotional Richness & Genuine emotion and human warmth, not a dry event log. \\
Engagement        & Dramatically meaningful and immersive as a campfire story. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{VLM extraction accuracy}

Stage~1 accuracy was evaluated across all 360 extracted frames (60 from the 5-min run, 120 from the 10-min run, 180 from the 15-min run), yielding 1{,}440 binary labels (360 frames $\times$ 4 conditions). Ground-truth labels were assigned as follows: all gameplay frames are expected to provide detectable location, describable action, visible entities, and visible player status (Ground Truth = 1). However, 75 of 360 frames (20.8\%) were identified as menu/inventory screens in which the game world is obscured by the UI overlay; for these frames, \texttt{location\_detectable} and \texttt{entities\_identified} ground truth was set to 0, since the environment and world entities are not directly observable.

\paragraph{Ground-truth labelling methodology.}
Labels were assigned by a deterministic rule, not by an LLM, to avoid any circularity between the judge and the system under evaluation. Menu and inventory frames were identified by matching the VLM-extracted \texttt{recent\_action} string against a fixed keyword list (\textit{inventory, recipe, crafting, menu, browsing, hovering}). When any keyword matched, the frame was classified as a UI-overlay frame and GT was set to 0 for \texttt{location\_detectable} and \texttt{entities\_identified}. All remaining frames received GT = 1 for all four conditions. To verify this classifier, 30 frames were randomly sampled (15 keyword-matched menu frames and 15 gameplay frames) and visually inspected by the author; all 30 were confirmed to match their assigned ground-truth label, yielding a 100\% spot-check agreement rate. This human verification step confirms that the keyword rule accurately captures the visual distinction between menu-obscured and open-world frames.

\subsection{Dialogue quality via dual LLM-as-judge}

The script \texttt{src/dialogue\_evaluator.py} implements an automated judge using the Adams dimensions above. Two independent judge models were used to cross-validate findings:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Gemini 2.5 Flash}~\cite{GeminiTeam2024b} (Google) --- the same model family as the generator, providing a within-family baseline.
  \item \textbf{Llama-3.3-70B via Groq}~\cite{Dubey2024} (Meta) --- a different model family, providing an independent cross-family validation.
\end{itemize}

For each run, the judge loads a slimmed version of the scene graph (narrative fields only: \texttt{timeline\_summary}, \texttt{environment}, entity names, event descriptions, and \texttt{player\_arc}) and the generated dialogue, then scores all seven Adams dimensions on a 1--5 scale. The judge also returns a list of \textbf{hallucinated entities}---proper nouns present in the dialogue but absent from the scene graph.

\section{Observation}

\noindent\textbf{Key observations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Grounding is the weakest dimension in both judges.} Gemini scores it at 4.67 and Groq/Llama at 4.17---the lowest axis on the radar chart for both. This cross-model agreement validates Grounding as a genuine pipeline weakness, not an artefact of any single judge.
  \item \textbf{Perfect agreement on Coherence and Arbitrary Content.} Both judges assign 5.00/5.00 to these dimensions across all runs, providing the strongest reliability signal in the evaluation. These are the most defensible findings.
  \item \textbf{Groq/Llama is consistently more conservative.} Across all dimensions, Llama scores at or below Gemini, with the largest gap on Credibility ($|\Delta|=0.67$). This systematic conservatism is consistent with known differences in how different model families calibrate evaluative leniency.
  \item \textbf{Campfire hallucination.} The word \emph{fire} was flagged as a hallucinated entity in the 5-minute Run~2 (064300). This is a systematic artefact of the story prompt framing (``NPC companion at a campfire''), which causes the model to reference fire when no campfire appears in the Minecraft footage.
  \item \textbf{Context length effect.} The 10-minute and 15-minute runs achieved perfect Grounding (5.00) from the Gemini judge with zero hallucinations, while the 5-minute runs averaged 4.00. This suggests richer gameplay context produces more accurately grounded narratives.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What the results mean collectively}

Taken together, the three evaluation methods---automated F1, dual LLM judges, and human raters---tell a consistent story. The pipeline reliably extracts structured information from gameplay screenshots (Stage~1 F1 of 0.85--1.00) and produces dialogue that is coherent, free of arbitrary UI content, and non-repetitive. These are the dimensions where all evaluators agree, and they represent the strongest validated claims of the project.

The more interesting finding is where the evaluators \emph{diverge}. Figure~\ref{fig:three_way} shows the full three-way comparison across all seven dimensions. Emotional Richness stands out as the largest gap: human raters scored it at 3.33 on average, while Gemini gave 5.00 and Groq gave 4.50. This gap is not a calibration artefact---it reflects a fundamental limitation of LLM-based judges for creative text. Automated judges can detect emotional vocabulary (words like \emph{heart stopped}, \emph{worried}, \emph{relief}) but cannot assess whether that warmth feels \emph{genuine} to a domain-aware human reader who knows what \emph{Minecraft} actually feels like to play.

\subsection{Implications for LLM-as-judge evaluation}

This project used two judges from different model families specifically to detect same-model leniency bias. The approach works: Groq/Llama is systematically more conservative than Gemini across all dimensions, with the largest gap on Credibility ($|\Delta|=0.67$). However, both automated judges still overestimate Emotional Richness relative to humans. This suggests that the problem is not model-family leniency but a deeper limitation: LLM judges share a common evaluation prior for emotional language that does not align with the expectations of a domain expert. A practical implication for future work is to move toward a \textbf{majority-vote multi-agent judge} using three or more models from different families (e.g., Gemini, Llama, GPT-4o, Claude). When the majority agree on a score, confidence in that score is higher; when they disagree strongly on a dimension, that disagreement itself is a signal that the dimension may require human calibration.

\subsection{Broader applicability of the architecture}

The pipeline as designed is game-agnostic. The only game-specific element is the input frame format: any game that produces sequential screenshots can feed Stage~1 without modification. Candidate domains include open-world RPGs (where NPC dialogue currently relies on branching trees), strategy games (where an AI narrator could describe the player's campaign decisions), and simulation games such as \emph{The Sims} or \emph{Stardew Valley} (where emergent player behaviour is currently unacknowledged by NPCs). The JSON scene graph produced at Stage~2 is also a natural integration point for existing game dialogue engines: rather than generating text, the pipeline could export the scene graph directly into a dialogue management system that selects from pre-written lines, grounding the selection in actual gameplay context.

\subsection{Towards real-time deployment}

The current implementation is a batch pipeline: it processes a fixed set of frames after a gameplay session ends. A natural extension is a \textbf{rolling-window variant} that processes the last $N$ frames on demand---for example, triggered when the player enters a dialogue zone. Preliminary estimation suggests that at 5-second frame intervals, a 5-minute context window (60 frames) could be processed in 30--60 seconds on the free-tier Gemini API, which may be acceptable for dialogue that appears between gameplay segments. Reducing the polling interval or caching scene graph updates frame-by-frame would bring this closer to real-time.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}

Frame order is user-supplied (e.g., filename order), not inferred from video timestamps. VLM mistakes propagate into the graph and dialogue; consolidation may over- or under-merge events. The campfire framing in the story prompt causes a systematic hallucination of \emph{fire} in environments where no campfire appears; rephrasing the NPC context to be environment-neutral would address this. The human study involved only two raters, each scoring one dialogue per duration group; a larger study with 3--5 raters per sample and inter-rater agreement metrics (e.g., Cohen's $\kappa$) would yield more statistically robust conclusions. Although using two judges from different model families (Gemini and Llama) substantially mitigates same-model leniency bias, both LLM judges still overestimate Emotional Richness relative to humans; future work should design a finer-grained emotional rubric or use pairwise preference judgements to better align automated scores with human perception. The pipeline does not yet integrate live game APIs or native scene graphs from engines; extending to engine telemetry would tighten grounding further.
